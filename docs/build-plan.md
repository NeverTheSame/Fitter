## Scope and assumptions

Fitter v1 is a 2D photo try-on web app: the user uploads a selfie, supplies a garment by URL or screenshot, and gets back a realistic image of themselves wearing it, generated by a hosted try-on API and served from managed Kubernetes.

**Decided in the Q&A (27 Oct 2026)**

| Decision | Choice | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reference text for the architecture | *AI Engineering* (Chip Huyen, O'Reilly 2025), chapter 10 "AI Engineering Architecture and User Feedback" | Every pattern in that chapter (context construction, guardrails, router, gateway, caching, orchestration, observability, feedback) gets a concrete container or component |
| Try-on fidelity | 2D photo (one realistic image, not multi-view or a 3D avatar) | Industry-standard approach; every hosted API supports it; 3D stays a later option |

## Market landscape: who exists, who pays, what works

The defining fact of this market in 2026 is that Google gives photo try-on away for free inside Search and Shopping, in Canada since late 2025, so a paid app cannot sell "see this listing on me" and has to sell what Google does not: garments outside Google's catalogue (social screenshots, thrift finds, any URL), a persistent wardrobe, honest no-retention privacy, and a frictionless first run. The technology is a commodity at $0.04–$0.075 per image; the business is UX, trust and retention.

**Audience and demand.** The evidence is mostly vendor-reported, but consistent: mobile-first, young, female-skewing fashion shoppers; [DRESSX](https://dressx.com/news/dressx-virtual-try-on-lifts-fashion-conversion-50-wwd-reports) reports 70% of try-on interactions on smartphones, try-on users 3× more likely to add to cart and 44% still active after 30 days versus 1% of non-users; Google says try-on images are shared far more than plain listings. Three use cases: deciding what to buy (now Google's), sharing for fun (Doppl, Snap; did not sustain a product), and planning outfits from what you own (Alta, Fits; where independent apps have gravitated). No independent demographic breakdown exists.

**What users complain about** (hands-on reports; store review text was not retrievable): body distortion (thinner, taller, "bobblehead"), misaligned trousers, a long dress cut to the length of the original outfit, slow generation (Doji's 30-minute avatar), monthly caps and throttling, watermarks and ads on free tiers, no fit or size information (every product disclaims it), and photos stored on servers with human review (Doppl). Incumbents sit at 3.0–3.7 stars; the bar is low and specific: keep the real body and face, handle bottoms and dresses, render in seconds, say plainly it is a look not a fit, and make the first three tries free of friction.

**Retailer impact numbers** are almost all from vendors ([Zeekit](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/363312/walmart-adds-virtual-try-on-tech.html?edition=122379) returns −36%, DRESSX conversion +50%, [Catches](https://noqta.tn/en/news/ai-virtual-try-on-startups-fashion-retail-returns-2026) 2.3× conversion). The only retailer-audited figure is [Zalando's](https://corporate.zalando.com/sites/default/files/media-download/zalando-se_shaping-the-future_ai-and-zalando.pdf) −8% size-related returns from measurement-based size advice, not photo try-on. Fitter should never claim reduced returns.

**The gap Fitter can take.** "Paste a URL" alone is not a gap (Doji, TryOnCam and Vybe do it; Doppl took screenshots). The defensible combination is: any garment from anywhere (screenshot of a TikTok, a thrift rack, a friend's photo) in one tap from the share sheet; a wardrobe that remembers garments and lets the user combine them; no retention of the selfie beyond the session unless they opt in; and markets where Google's feature is not live. The pricing section builds on the observed anchors: 10 free credits is the norm, $4.99–$6.99 a week is the long-tail pattern, and $8–16 a month for 200 renders is the freemium pattern.

## How they build it: the technology under the hood

Every shipped 2D try-on since 2023 is a diffusion inpainting model that takes a "clothing-agnostic" version of the person, pose and body cues, and the garment as a reference, and paints the garment on; newer models drop the masks and pose inputs, and Google's own Shopping try-on now runs on a general image editor (Nano Banana). A hosted API hides four pre-processing models from you, but you still own selfie gating, garment classification, re-rolls and caching.

**Model lineage (what a hosted engine is built from)**

| Model | Year, venue | Backbone and idea | Licence | Why it matters to Fitter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [TryOnDiffusion](https://research.google/pubs/tryondiffusion-a-tale-of-two-u-nets/) (Google) | CVPR 2023 | "Parallel-UNet": garment warped implicitly through cross-attention; takes a photo of the garment worn by someone else | Not released | Ancestor of Google's Shopping try-on; proved on-model garment photos work as input |
| [OOTDiffusion](https://github.com/levihsu/OOTDiffusion) | AAAI 2025 | SD inpainting + OpenPose + human parsing; needs a category flag (upper, lower, dress) | Licence file not named in README | On Replicate at \~3 min/image; too slow |
| [IDM-VTON](https://github.com/yisol/idm-vton) | ECCV 2024 | SDXL + IP-Adapter; needs DensePose, OpenPose, ATR/LIP parsing, agnostic mask | CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 (non-commercial) | Most popular open model (1.6M Replicate runs) but unusable commercially |
| [CatVTON](https://github.com/Zheng-Chong/CatVTON) | ICLR 2025 | SD 1.5 inpainting, 50M trainable params, runs in under 8 GB VRAM; mask-free and FLUX variants followed | CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 (non-commercial) | Same licence problem |

| [FitDiT](https://github.com/BoyuanJiang/FitDiT) | Nov 2024 | Diffusion Transformer, 1152×1536, user-adjustable mask | CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 | Commercial users are sent to Tencent Cloud |
| [Any2AnyTryon](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.15891v1), Voost | ICCV 2025, Aug 2025 | Mask-free, pose-free DiT models | Research | Where the field is heading: fewer pre-processing steps |

| [Nano Banana](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/shopping/studio-quality-digital-try-on/) (Gemini 2.5 Flash Image, now 2.1) | 2025–2026 | General instruction-following image editor; selfie + garment + a precise instruction | Hosted, commercial | What Google Shopping ships on since Dec 2025; the general-editor route |

**The pre-processing a hosted API hides.** The open research stack needs DensePose and OpenPose for body and pose, SCHP or ATR/LIP for human parsing, an agnostic mask that erases the current clothing, and a garment category. That is four models before the diffusion step, each with its own licence to check (DensePose comes from Detectron2; SCHP is a research repo). FASHN, Kling and Vertex do all of it server-side and expose only knobs: `category`, `garment_photo_type` (flat-lay vs on-model; a brand label on a flat-lay tee transfers "pixel-perfectly"), `cover_feet`, `adjust_hands`, `restore_background`, `restore_clothes`, `long_top`, `num_samples` 1–4 and `seed` ([FASHN endpoints](https://docs.fashn.ai/fashn-api/endpoints), [FASHN results guide](https://fashn.ai/blog/getting-the-best-virtual-try-on-results-part-i)).

**What the app still owns.** Selfie gating: exactly one person, face visible, full or half body, standing, simple pose, fitted clothes, nothing hidden by hands or hair ([Kling's input rules](https://kling.ai/quickstart/ai-virtual-try-on-guide) are the clearest published list). Garment gating: one item, classified into tops, bottoms or one-pieces (plus shoes and accessories only on Try-On Max or Nano Banana), with the flat-lay or on-model flag set. Re-rolls: generate 2–4 samples and let the user pick, which is how Vertex (4 samples a call) and FASHN (`num_samples`) expect to be used. Caching by hash of selfie, garment and parameters.

**Documented failure classes and the mitigation each engine or product uses**

| Failure | Seen in | Mitigation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hands and fingers | All diffusion models; FASHN v1.6 changelog lists a "previously missing finger" fix | `adjust_hands`; prefer poses with hands visible and apart from the body |
| Small text, logos, fine prints | Kling, Fora Soft, FASHN v1.5 | Flat-lay flag; show the product photo beside the render; consider Nano Banana for graphic-heavy garments and A/B per category |
| Body shape and skin tone drift toward the garment's model | OutfitAnyone, Doji, Doppl ("bobblehead") | `garment_photo_type: model` so the engine knows to ignore the source body; identity check in output guardrails |
| Background leakage from the garment photo | OutfitAnyone | Background removal on flat-lay crops; `restore_background` |
| Bottoms-only garments | Kling ("not recommended"), Doppl (misaligned trousers) | Require a full-body selfie for bottoms; route bottoms to the engine that handles them best in the bake-off |
| Feet and shoes under long garments | FASHN (preserved shoes under a gown) | `cover_feet` when category is one-piece and length is long |
| Layering (top over bottom) | Doppl, FASHN v1.5 | One garment per request; sequential try-on for outfits |
| Dress length forced to the original outfit | Doppl | `long_top` and one-piece routing; accept it as a known limit in the UI copy |

**Why 2D won over 3D.** 3D try-on needs a purpose-built asset for every SKU ($50–$150 by photogrammetry, $200–$500 by hand per a [vendor guide](https://www.photta.app/business/guides/photo-vs-3d-try-on)), a body capture, and cloth physics that is "often still visually unconvincing". A "paste any URL" app can never obtain a 3D asset for an arbitrary retailer's product; 2D diffusion works from the retailer's existing product photo and one user photo. Google, Kling, FASHN and the whole app ecosystem shipped 2D for that reason; diffusion "optimizes for looking right, not fitting right" ([Fora Soft](https://www.forasoft.com/blog/article/virtual-try-on-development)), which is why none of them promise fit.

**Self-hosting as a reference point.** Leffa at \~6 s on an A100 is about $0.008 of raw compute per image at [Replicate's](https://replicate.com/pricing) $0.0014/s, roughly a tenth of FASHN's $0.075. But you pay for idle GPU hours and multi-gigabyte cold starts, you reproduce the four-model pre-processing chain, and you own uptime. Below a few thousand images a month the hosted API wins on total cost; above tens of thousands, a Leffa (MIT) + SAM 2 (Apache 2.0) + BiRefNet (MIT) stack on RunPod or Hugging Face Endpoints becomes the fourth adapter behind the gateway, not a rewrite.

**Why FASHN first despite not being cheapest.** It is the only vendor that documents category and photo-type auto-detection (which removes two classifiers from your pipeline), a retention window, rate limits, a status page, SDKs and an `llms.txt` for coding agents. The one independent side-by-side test in 2026 ([Fuser, 28 Sep 2026](https://fuser.studio/articles/best-ai-virtual-try-on)) found v1.6 "changed the least" and kept face, hair, jeans and backdrop while swapping a flat-lay shirt. The premium over fal's $0.04 endpoint is $35 a month at 1,000 images and $350 at 10,000: cheap insurance in a consumer app where a bad render is a lost user. FASHN raised its price from $0.04 to $0.075 on 1 Mar 2025 ([pricing update](https://fashn.ai/blog/pricing-update-for-developer-api)), so budget on $0.075, not on the tier price.

**Before committing, run a bake-off (under $15).** 20–50 of your own selfie + screenshot pairs through FASHN v1.6 (performance and balanced), fal image-apps-v2 and Nano Banana 2.1. Score each on face preserved, garment print preserved, body shape unchanged, background unchanged. No neutral benchmark exists; every published ranking is a vendor blog or a single-run test.

**Integration shape.** FASHN is `POST /v1/run` → poll `GET /v1/status/{id}` or a webhook, inputs as URL or data URI, output as CDN URL or base64. fal is a queue with an optional `sync_mode`. Build the adapter against FASHN's request shape and keep a second adapter for fal; failover becomes a config switch in the router.

**Unverified, so not relied on:** Kling's units per image; Alibaba Model Studio try-on (all English docs now redirect, possibly withdrawn internationally); Vmake API pricing; OpenAI gpt-image-2.5 per-image cost (token-priced only, gpt-image-1 no longer listed); fal and Replicate sign-up credit amounts; whether fal's $0.04 endpoint is a rebadged FASHN or Kling model.

## Garment input pipeline: URL, screenshot, and the law

Treat the URL path as a convenience that works most of the time and the screenshot as the path that always works: fetch the page once at the user's request, take the product image from structured data, let a cheap vision model pick and classify it, strip the background only for flat-lays, keep nothing after the render, and fall back to "take a screenshot" the moment a retailer blocks you. This is the posture Google chose for Doppl (screenshots only) and it is the lowest-risk reading of Canadian and US scraping law.

&#91;embedded content: garment acquisition · 4 fetch attempts, 1 universal fallback\]

Every fetch attempt is free at Fitter's early volume; the only paid step is the vision call, and both entry paths converge on it, so a blocked retailer costs the user one extra tap, not a failed try-on.

**The fallback chain, cheapest first**

| Step | How | Cost | When it fails |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. **Retailer reality.** [Zara](https://apify.com/studio-amba/zara-scraper) and [H&M](https://scrape.do/industries/ecommerce/hm-scraper/) sit behind Akamai and return 403 to anything that is not a real browser; H&M embeds the full JSON-LD `ProductGroup` once you are through. Cloudflare has [blocked identified AI crawlers by default since 1 Jul 2025](https://www.technologyreview.com/2025/07/01/1119498/cloudflare-will-now-by-default-block-ai-bots-from-crawling-its-clients-websites/). Shein, Aritzia, Lululemon and Uniqlo were not individually verified; assume PerimeterX-style challenges and test each in week 2. Expect steps 1–3 to succeed on most Shopify and mid-size stores and to fail on the two biggest fast-fashion sites, which is exactly where the screenshot path earns its place.

**What the law says, in short**

| Jurisdiction | Ruling or statement | Effect on Fitter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| US | [hiQ v. LinkedIn](https://torys.com/our-latest-thinking/publications/2022/01/the-screen-scraping-risk-landscape-in-north-america): scraping public pages is not a CFAA crime; but hiQ [lost on breach of contract](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/hiq-and-linkedin-reach-proposed-settlement-in-landmark-scraping-case) and paid $500,000 (Dec 2022) | Never log in, never create accounts, never agree to a retailer's terms |
| US | [Meta v. Bright Data](https://www.fbm.com/business-litigation/publications/major-decision-affects-law-of-scraping-and-online-data-collection-meta-platforms-v-bright-data/) (N.D. | Canada | [Trader v. CarGurus](https://torys.com/our-latest-thinking/publications/2022/01/the-screen-scraping-risk-landscape-in-north-america) (2017 ONSC): scraping and re-posting a competitor's product photos infringed copyright | Never store, re-host or display retailer photos beyond the render session; link back to the source |
| Canada | [OPC statement, 28 Oct 2024](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-news/speeches-and-statements/2024/js-dc_20241028/): public data is still protected; "contractual terms alone do not make scraping lawful"; [MLT Aikins](https://www.mltaikins.com/insights/understanding-copyright-and-privacy-issues-related-to-data-scraping/) notes courts frown most on bypassing technical measures; CanLII v. Caseway (BCSC) pending | No unblockers, no catalogue building, no crawling |

**The risk ladder Fitter stands on.** Lowest: the user uploads a screenshot they took. Low: the server fetches one product page's own `og:image` at the user's request, uses it for one render, shows the source link, deletes it. Medium: proxied fetches that defeat Akamai or Cloudflare. High: crawling catalogues or re-hosting product photos. Fitter ships at "low" with "lowest" as the fallback and never climbs higher without legal advice. The generated render is the user's own image; the garment photo is transient input.

**Privacy of the selfie.** The selfie is the sensitive asset, not the garment. It goes to the try-on API as a short-lived signed URL or base64 (FASHN deletes base64 inputs on completion and CDN outputs after 3 days per its [retention page](https://docs.fashn.ai/api-overview/data-retention-privacy)), EXIF and GPS are stripped before upload, and Fitter's own copy is deleted at the end of the session unless the user saves it to a wardrobe. Say so on the upload screen; Doppl's server-side storage with human review was a listed complaint.

## AI-native architecture: chapter 10 mapped onto Fitter

Every component the chapter adds to the bare "query → model → response" flow has a named home in Fitter: nine containers on one small Kubernetes node, two hosted free tiers for traces and analytics, and the try-on engines as external APIs behind your own gateway. The chapter's order is also the build order: direct model call first, then guardrails, then gateway and router, then caches, then the agentic garment-fetch loop, with observability wired in from day one and the feedback loop closing it ([author's chapter summary](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chiphuyen/aie-book/main/chapter-summaries.md); the architecture half is the author's own ["Building A Generative AI Platform"](https://huyenchip.com/2024/07/25/genai-platform.html), which the chapter expands).

&#91;embedded content: Fitter services · 9 in-cluster containers, 5 outside services\]

The worker is the only thing that orchestrates; it never calls an engine or a model directly, only `guard`, `tryon-gateway` and `llm-gateway`, so every paid call is routed, cached, budgeted and traced in one place. `api`, `worker` and `tryon-gateway` all read and write Postgres and Valkey; those lines are left out of the picture for legibility.

&#91;embedded content: try-on request flow · 3 decisions, 2 loops\]

Two of the three exits before the router cost nothing, which is the economic reason the chapter's order matters: guardrails and the cache run before the only paid step, and the two loops (retry inside a request, learning across nights) both feed back into the router.

**A try-on request, step by step**

1. `web` uploads the selfie straight to object storage with a signed URL from `api`; `api` writes a `job` row and pushes the id to the queue.
2. `worker` picks it up and opens a trace. Garment acquisition runs first: URL → fetch → candidate images → vision judge picks the product image and classifies it → background removed → perceptual hash looked up in Postgres; a hit reuses the canonical garment.
3. `guard` input checks run in parallel: moderation on both images, face count, EXIF strip. A fail ends the job with a user-facing reason and costs nothing.
4. `tryon-gateway` checks the exact cache. A hit returns in milliseconds. A miss runs the router: category, photo type, user tier and engine health select an engine; the adapter calls it; the cost is written to the ledger as the span closes.
5. `guard` output checks: moderation, face similarity above threshold, judge score. Below threshold → retry with a new seed once, then the fallback engine; still failing → the user gets a clear message and the credit back.
6. The render is stored, the job marked done, `web` is notified over SSE. Every click afterwards (regenerate, download, share, thumbs) is an event that scores this render.
7. Nightly, `eval` samples the day's renders, re-judges them, compares engines, and raises a drift alert if any engine's score moves more than a set margin.

**What the author says to skip, and what Fitter skips in v1.** "A component can be skipped if your system works well without it." v1 ships with the direct FASHN call, `guard`, the exact cache and traces. The router starts as a five-line rules table, the semantic (garment-dedup) cache and `eval` arrive in week 3, Hatchet only if plain retries prove insufficient, and no orchestration framework at all. Each addition is a new way to fail, so each one lands with its own metric.

**Where the 2026 tooling has moved.** LLM Guard is archived and unmaintained; Guardrails AI ends hosted inference on 25 Aug 2026; both are out. NeMo Guardrails (Apache 2.0) is text-centric and adds little to an image app, so Fitter's guard is a thin service over free hosted moderation plus a CPU face model. No published source applies this chapter to image pipelines; the mapping above is this document's, built on image-capable tools that do exist.

## Unit economics and pricing model

A try-on costs Fitter about $0.10 all-in on FASHN (about $0.06 on fal), so sell credit packs and capped monthly plans at $0.09–$0.18 per try-on, give three free try-ons for life on the cheapest engine, and watch the free tier rather than the cluster: at a 3% conversion rate the free try-ons cost more than one Pack per payer brings in. Google is free, so the paid product must be the wardrobe and the "any garment from anywhere" workflow, not the render.

**Three rules that keep the margins real.** First, the router sends free and Pack users to the $0.04 engine and Closet and Studio users to FASHN, so the expensive engine is only paid for by people who paid. Second, a regeneration costs a credit after the first free re-roll per garment; unlimited re-rolls are how Fitroom's economics break. Third, Stripe's C$0.30 fixed fee means nothing is sold below C$9.99.

**Break-even, per 100 sign-ups.** Three free try-ons each on the fal engine cost about 100 × 3 × $0.055 = $16.50. At a 3% conversion rate, three payers each buy a Pack routed to fal, contributing $4.38 apiece (C$9.99 ≈ $7.20, less \~$0.42 Stripe and \~$2.40 of renders at full use), so $13.14 in total. One Pack per payer does not repay the free tier; about 1.3 Packs per payer, or a 4% conversion rate, does. On top of that, the cluster's \~$36 a month needs about eight more Packs a month and FASHN Tier I another four or five. Conversion and repeat purchase are the two numbers that decide this business, and both are a share-sheet and TikTok problem, not an engineering one.

**Second revenue line, when the consumer funnel proves thin.** Every durable business in the landscape section is B2B: a Shopify app at $49 a month for 250 renders (A&A's exact price) sells the same `tryon-gateway` and `guard` to merchants with no change to the pipeline. Keep the API contract of `tryon-gateway` clean enough to expose with a key.

**What to measure from day one** so these assumptions become numbers: renders per try-on, cache hit rate, free-to-paid conversion, utilisation of monthly caps, engine cost per paying user per month, and regenerate rate per engine (the implicit-feedback signal from the architecture section).

## Step-by-step build plan

Six phases, each ending in a gate you can verify, in the order the chapter adds components: a working direct call first, then guardrails, then gateway and router, then product and payments, then Kubernetes, then hardening. Budget about 4–6 weeks part-time with an AI coding assistant. Every phase lists what to build, what to verify, and what "done" means.

No phase starts until the previous gate is met; Kubernetes arrives in week 5 on purpose, once there is something worth deploying and every component already emits the metrics the cluster's alerts depend on.

### Phase 0 — Accounts and the bake-off (days 1–2)

1. Create accounts: [FASHN](https://fashn.ai/pricing) ($7.50 minimum, 100 credits), [fal.ai](https://fal.ai/pricing), [Google AI Studio](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing) (Gemini key), OpenAI (moderation only, free), Cloudflare (free), Azure (new account for the $200 and 12-month free services), GitHub, Langfuse Hobby, PostHog, Clerk, Neon, Upstash, Resend. Stripe comes in Phase 4.
2. Collect a bake-off set: 20 selfies that cover full-body and half-body, two body types, two skin tones, a seated pose and a group photo (the last two must be rejected later), and 20 garments: 8 on-model retailer shots, 8 flat-lays, 4 screenshots with page chrome. Get written consent from anyone who is not you.
3. Write `scripts/bakeoff.py`: for every pair, call FASHN v1.6 (performance and balanced), fal `image-apps-v2/virtual-try-on`, and Nano Banana 2.1 with a fixed prompt; save outputs and latency; cost is under $15.
4. Score each render 0–2 on four axes: face preserved, body shape unchanged, garment print and colour preserved, background unchanged. Record the sheet in `docs/bakeoff-2026-10.md`.

Gate: an engine ranking with numbers, and the routing table's first version (which engine for tops, bottoms, one-pieces, flat-lay vs on-model).

### Phase 1 — The direct call, end to end (week 1)

1. Scaffold the monorepo above; `deploy/compose.yaml` runs Valkey, Postgres 16 with `pgvector`, MinIO and Bifrost.
2. `packages/schemas`: `TryOnJob`, `Garment`, `Render`, `Feedback`, `CostEvent`; generate the Zod side from the Pydantic side.
3. `services/api`: `POST /uploads/sign` (presigned PUT to MinIO/R2), `POST /tryons` (writes a job row, pushes to a Valkey stream), `GET /tryons/{id}` and an SSE endpoint for status.
4. `services/worker`: `pipeline.py` with four typed steps: `acquire_garment` (Phase 2 stub: accept an uploaded image), `check_inputs` (stub), `generate` (direct FASHN call: `POST /v1/run`, poll `/v1/status/{id}`, `category=auto`, `garment_photo_type=auto`, `num_samples=1`), `check_output` (stub), `persist`. Each step emits an OpenTelemetry span with `cost_usd` as an attribute.
5. `apps/web`: three screens: upload selfie, add garment (image upload only for now), result with a regenerate button. Install the Langfuse and OTel SDKs now, not later; the chapter's rule is observability from the beginning.
6. Write ADR-001 (hosted engine, not self-hosted), ADR-002 (2D, not 3D), ADR-003 (no orchestration framework).

Gate: selfie + garment image to a render in the browser locally, one trace per job in Langfuse showing every step and its cost.

### Phase 2 — Context and guardrails (week 2)

1. Garment acquisition in `services/worker/garment/`: `fetch.py` implements the fallback chain (Shopify JSON, direct fetch with JSON-LD and `og:image`, Jina Reader, Firecrawl); each attempt is a span; an allow-list of schemes and a block-list of private IP ranges guard against SSRF.
2. `pick.py`: one Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite call through Bifrost with the candidate thumbnails; returns `{index, category, photo_type, confidence}` as JSON. Put the prompt in `prompts/garment_pick.md` and a promptfoo suite beside it with 15 labelled cases.
3. `clean.py`: `rembg` with `isnet-general-use` for flat-lays, pad to 3:4 white; skip for on-model; `imagehash.phash` and a CLIP embedding into `garments` for de-duplication (the semantic cache).
4. Screenshot path: Gemini returns a bounding box; crop; same clean-up.
5. `services/guard`: `POST /check/input` runs OpenAI `omni-moderation` on both images, MediaPipe face detection (exactly one face, face height above 8% of image height), a body-visible heuristic from pose landmarks, and EXIF stripping; returns a structured `GuardVerdict` with a user-facing reason. `POST /check/output` runs moderation, InsightFace cosine similarity between selfie and render (threshold from the bake-off, start at 0.45), and a Gemini judge score 1–5 for garment match. Both endpoints emit metrics `guard_block_total{reason}`.
6. Worker: a failed input check ends the job before any paid call; a failed output check triggers the retry policy: new seed once, then the fallback engine, then a friendly failure with the credit refunded.
7. Test ten retailer URLs (two Shopify stores, Zara, H&M, Aritzia, Lululemon, Uniqlo, Shein, Amazon, a Poshmark listing) and record which step succeeded; expect Zara and H&M to need the screenshot path.

Gate: a URL becomes a clean garment in under 8 seconds on most sites; a group photo or a seated pose is rejected for $0; a bad render is retried and the trace shows why.

### Phase 3 — Gateway, router, caches, evaluation (week 3)

1. `services/tryon-gateway`: `POST /v1/tryon` with the `TryOnRequest` schema; `adapters/fashn.py`, `adapters/fal.py`, `adapters/nanobanana.py` behind one `Engine` protocol (`submit`, `poll`, `cost`); the worker now calls this instead of FASHN directly.
2. `router.py`: reads `routing_rules` from Postgres (category, photo type, user tier, feature flags) and the circuit-breaker state from Valkey; returns an ordered engine list. Seed it with the Phase 0 table.
3. `cache.py`: exact cache key `sha256(selfie) + sha256(garment_canonical) + engine + params` in Valkey with a 30-day TTL and a per-user namespace; a hit skips the engine and records `cost_usd=0`.
4. `ledger.py`: every engine call writes a `cost_events` row (user, engine, usd, latency); per-user daily and monthly budgets; a provider concurrency semaphore (FASHN default 6).
5. Circuit breaker: three failures or two timeouts in 60 seconds opens the breaker for 5 minutes and the router skips that engine; alert on open.
6. Bifrost config: virtual keys per service, monthly budget per key, Prometheus metrics on, semantic cache off (not useful for image calls).
7. `services/eval`: a CronJob at 03:00 that samples 50 renders from the day, re-judges them (identity, garment match), writes per-engine scores, and posts a Langfuse score and a Grafana annotation; drift alert when a 7-day mean moves more than 0.3. Add DeepEval image metrics to the same job.
8. Test failover: set an invalid FASHN key, submit a try-on, confirm the trace shows FASHN failing, the breaker opening, fal succeeding.

Gate: all engine calls go through the gateway; a repeated try-on costs $0; the nightly eval produces a per-engine score; failover works without code changes.

### Phase 4 — Product, feedback and payments (week 4)

1. Auth: Clerk (free to 50,000 users) with Next.js middleware and a JWT check in `api`; store `clerk_user_id` only.
2. Wardrobe: save garments (canonical crop plus source link, never the retailer page) and renders the user explicitly keeps; everything else is deleted by a nightly retention job after 7 days (renders) and at session end (selfies not saved to a profile).
3. Feedback: thumbs with a one-tap reason on every render; implicit events (`regenerate`, `download`, `share`, `save`, `delete`, `abandon`) sent to PostHog and mirrored as Langfuse scores keyed by trace id. Comparative view when the judge score is below 3: two renders side by side, randomised order, "which looks more like you?".
4. Billing: Stripe Checkout for the Pack and Closet plans, webhook to `api` that credits the ledger with an idempotency key; credits decrement on successful render only.
5. PWA: manifest, service worker, Web Share Target so a screenshot can be shared straight into Fitter from the iPhone share sheet; camera capture with `capture="user"`.
6. Copy: "a visualisation, not a fit" under every render; the privacy statement on the upload screen (what is sent where, what is deleted when).

Gate: a new user signs up, uses three free try-ons, pays C$9.99, gets 40 credits, and every click is visible as a score on the trace.

### Phase 5 — Managed Kubernetes (week 5)

1. `infra/terraform/azure`: resource group in Canada Central, AKS Free tier, one node pool with `Standard_B2pls_v2`, system node pool shared, OIDC issuer on, a storage account (or skip it and use R2), Cloudflare DNS records. Check the current AKS outbound options before applying: if an outbound type without a Standard Load Balancer is available for your cluster shape, use it; otherwise accept the $18.25 LB for now and record it in the ADR. Keep `infra/terraform/aws` as an EKS module with the same variables so the move is a `terraform apply`, not a redesign.
2. `deploy/helm/fitter`: one chart with a sub-template per container from the containers table; resource requests exactly as listed there; `PodDisruptionBudget` off (one node); `HorizontalPodAutoscaler` only on `worker`.
3. Ingress: a `cloudflared` Deployment running a Cloudflare Tunnel to `web` and `api`; no Ingress controller, no public IP for inbound; Cloudflare Turnstile on sign-up and on the free try-on endpoint.
4. Secrets: External Secrets Operator reading Azure Key Vault, or Sealed Secrets if you want zero extra cost; never a plain Secret in git.
5. CI: GitHub Actions builds one image per service on PR, runs promptfoo and unit tests, pushes to GHCR on merge to `main`, then `helm upgrade --install` with the environment values; add Argo CD only if you want the GitOps demo. Tag every deploy with a Grafana annotation; that is the CFR counter.
6. Observability: OpenTelemetry Collector Deployment exporting traces to Langfuse and metrics to Grafana Cloud; four alerts: try-on success rate below 95% over 15 minutes, p95 latency above 30 seconds, any circuit breaker open, nightly eval score drift. Alert time stamps give MTTD; incident notes in `docs/incidents/` give MTTR.
7. Data: Neon for Postgres with `pgvector`; Upstash or in-cluster Valkey (in-cluster is fine on 4 GiB and keeps the queue local); R2 with a lifecycle rule.

Gate: `https://fitter.<your-domain>` serves the app from AKS; a merge to `main` deploys in under 10 minutes; `helm rollback` works; the monthly cost estimate in the Azure portal matches the budget section within $10.

### Phase 6 — Hardening and launch (week 6)

1. Rate limits per user and per IP in `api` (Valkey token bucket); per-provider concurrency already in the gateway.
2. Load test: 20 concurrent try-ons for 10 minutes with k6; confirm the queue drains, FASHN concurrency is respected, no OOM on the node.
3. Re-run the Phase 0 bake-off through the production path; compare scores to the originals; this is the regression test for the whole pipeline.
4. Legal pages: terms, privacy (what the try-on vendor receives, retention windows, deletion on request), and the "we fetch one product image at your request and keep nothing" statement for retailers.
5. Delete-my-data endpoint that removes wardrobe, renders, ledger rows and emails FASHN's legal address with prediction ids if a user asks for upstream deletion.
6. Launch: share-sheet demo video, a Product Hunt or TikTok post, and a weekly review of the five metrics from the unit-economics section. Decide on the Closet annual plan and the B2B Shopify app only after four weeks of data.

Gate: a week of real users with no alert left unexplained, an incident log with MTTD and MTTR, and a cost per paying user you can state.

## Risks, open questions, and decisions deferred

The two risks that can end the project are market, not engineering: Google gives the core feature away, and the standalone-app track record is poor. The engineering risks are all bounded by the gateway and the screenshot fallback.
